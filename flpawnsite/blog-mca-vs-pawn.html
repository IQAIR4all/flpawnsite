What Is a Merchant Cash Advance?

A merchant cash advance isn't technically a loan. The MCA company purchases a portion of your future credit card or debit card sales at a discount — paying you a lump sum today in exchange for a larger amount pulled from your daily revenue until the balance is repaid.

The repayment happens automatically. Every day, the MCA company takes a percentage of your card sales — called the "holdback rate" — directly from your merchant account. This continues until the full purchased amount is collected. There's no fixed end date; repayment speeds up when your sales are strong and slows when they're slow.

On paper, that flexibility sounds appealing. In practice, the FTC has taken action against MCA companies for deceptive and abusive practices, and the numbers behind these products are often far worse than borrowers realize when they sign.

Not regulated like a loan

Because MCAs are structured as the purchase of future receivables rather than a loan, they're largely exempt from state usury laws and federal lending regulations that cap interest rates. This is intentional — it allows MCA companies to charge rates that would be illegal if the product were classified as a loan.

The Real Cost of an MCA: Factor Rates Explained

MCA companies don't quote interest rates. They quote factor rates — a multiplier applied to the amount you receive. A factor rate of 1.4 means that for every $10,000 you borrow, you repay $14,000. A factor rate of 1.5 means you repay $15,000.

That sounds manageable until you account for time. When you convert a factor rate to an annualized percentage rate — the standard measure used for every other lending product — the numbers become alarming.

Red Flags in Every MCA Contract

Beyond the cost, MCA contracts contain clauses that give the lender extraordinary power over your business — power that most borrowers don't read carefully until it's too late.

🚩
Confession of Judgment (COJ)

Many MCA contracts include a confession of judgment clause, which allows the lender to obtain a court judgment against you without notifying you first. They can freeze your bank account before you've had a chance to dispute anything. Bloomberg's investigative reporting on COJ clauses exposed how widely this was being abused against small business owners.

🚩
Personal Guarantee

Even though an MCA is technically a business product, most contracts include a personal guarantee. If your business can't repay, your personal assets — savings, property, vehicles — are on the hook.

🚩
Stacking

MCA companies often allow — and encourage — taking a second or third advance before the first is repaid. Each new advance is taken at a new factor rate. Stacked MCAs can drain 30–50% of daily revenue, leaving nothing for payroll, rent, or inventory.

🚩
No Early Payoff Benefit

Unlike a traditional loan, paying off an MCA early saves you nothing. The factor rate is fixed — you owe the full repayment amount regardless of when you pay it back. Early payoff is a trap, not an incentive.

🚩
Default Provisions That Trigger Easily

MCA contracts define "default" broadly — sometimes including changing your bank account, taking on other financing, or revenue falling below a threshold. A technical default can allow the lender to demand the full remaining balance immediately.

The debt cycle is by design

The MCA industry's business model depends on repeat customers. FTC enforcement actions have documented how MCA companies pressure struggling borrowers into taking new advances to cover the payments on existing ones — a cycle that deepens debt rather than resolving it.

What the Regulators Are Saying

MCA companies have operated in a regulatory gray area for years, but that's changing. The FTC, CFPB, and several states have all taken action or proposed rules targeting deceptive MCA practices in recent years:

  • The FTC sued a major MCA company in 2022 for deceptive practices including falsely claiming no prepayment penalties and misrepresenting the total repayment amount.
  • The CFPB's small business lending rule will require lenders — including some MCA providers — to disclose pricing in a standardized way for the first time.
  • New York enacted legislation banning confessions of judgment against out-of-state defendants after Bloomberg's investigation exposed widespread abuse.
  • California now requires MCA companies to disclose an APR equivalent — the first state to do so.

That regulatory trend is a signal: MCA products are increasingly recognized as predatory, and the industry is under growing pressure to disclose costs and clean up its practices. In the meantime, the burden falls on small business owners to protect themselves.
